Methods and sources

Kolorist builds on established colour science and open standards. This page lists what the app uses, where it comes from, and what was developed for the app. The same explanations are in the app: tap “Method: …” below a section, or choose Methods and sources at the bottom of Palettes.

OKLab and OKLCH

A perceptual colour space where equal numeric steps are perceived as equally large colour differences. Kolorist computes gradients in OKLab, and tone scales, lighter/darker steps, harmonies and saturation in OKLCH (lightness, chroma, hue).

Source: Björn Ottosson: “A perceptual color space for image processing” (2020)

CSS Color Module Level 4

The standard for colour on the web. Kolorist uses the specification’s matrices and transfer functions for sRGB, Display P3, Adobe RGB, Lab and OKLab, its gamut mapping (chroma is reduced in OKLCH while lightness and hue are preserved) and its colour text syntax.

Source: W3C: CSS Color Module Level 4 (Candidate Recommendation)

CIELab, CIE LCH and Bradford adaptation

The CIE colour space based on human colour vision. Kolorist reports Lab and LCH with a D50 white point, like ICC profiles and Photoshop, and uses the Bradford transform between D65 and D50.

Source: CIE 15:2018 Colorimetry; K. M. Lam (1985), the Bradford transform

CIEDE2000 (ΔE00)

The CIE formula for perceived colour difference. Used when comparing colours, for gamut deviations and in the analysis of colours that become hard to tell apart with colour vision deficiency. The implementation is checked against the test data in Sharma et al.

Source: G. Sharma, W. Wu, E. N. Dalal: Color Research & Application 30(1), 2005; CIE 142-2001

WCAG 2.2 contrast

The W3C requirements for contrast between text/graphics and background, computed from relative luminance. “Fix” changes only lightness (OKLCH) until the requirement is met.

Source: W3C: Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2, success criteria 1.4.3, 1.4.6 and 1.4.11

Reading contrast by APCA

APCA (Accessible Perceptual Contrast Algorithm) was developed as the basis for contrast in WCAG 3, which is still a draft. It gives reading contrast as Lc from about −108 to 106 – positive for dark text on a light background, negative for light text on a dark one – and accounts for the eye perceiving light and dark colours differently. It follows perceived legibility better than WCAG 2, especially for dark and saturated colours.

How much is needed depends on font size and weight: Lc 90 is preferred and 75 the minimum for body text, 60 for larger text, 45 for large or bold headlines, 30 for non-essential text and 15 for icons and graphics. The contrast check shows Lc next to the WCAG requirements, with what the contrast is good for. Whether text on the app’s own colour fields is black or white is also decided with APCA, which gives white text on more mid-tones.

Accessibility regulations still refer to WCAG 2. Use APCA as a supplement; the WCAG 2.2 requirements are shown and calculated as before.

Source: Myndex: APCA (Accessible Perceptual Contrast Algorithm) 0.0.98G-4g

Colour vision deficiency simulation

A physiologically based model of protan, deutan and tritan deficiencies, with matrices in linear sRGB and severity as a blend with normal vision. Achromatopsia is shown as luminance alone. Used in Assess › Colour vision, the contrast preview and the camera filter. The simulation is an approximation; experience varies between people.

Source: G. M. Machado, M. M. Oliveira, L. A. F. Fernandes: IEEE TVCG 15(6), 2009

ICC profiles and colour management

Conversion between colour spaces via ICC profiles, with the chosen rendering intent. Performed by Apple’s colour management (ColorSync). Profiles are not bundled with the app; Kolorist uses the system’s profiles and profiles you import.

Source: International Colour Consortium (ICC.1); Apple ColorSync via Core Graphics

Clean CMYK values (UCR/GCR)

The common grey component of C, M and Y is moved to black, and Kolorist searches for the separation with the fewest inks that stays within 1 ΔE00 of the profile’s own separation.

Source: Established print technique; the search was developed for Kolorist

Colour wheels and harmonies

Complementary, split-complementary, analogous (also with a complementary accent), triad, square, double complementary and even distributions are computed as angles on the chosen colour wheel: OKLCH, CIE LCH, HSL, RYB, Munsell, Hering’s opponent-colour wheel or Goethe’s wheel from his Theory of Colours (1810). With Munsell, real Munsell colours are used in the steps of the book (hue 2.5, value 1, chroma 2). The RYB, Hering and Goethe wheels are piecewise linear mappings made for the app. The monochromatic harmony keeps the hue fixed and spreads the tones evenly along a line in lightness (OKLCH) and saturation, taken as a share of the highest chroma within the gamut at each lightness. Natural lightness order shifts lightness according to the hue’s own lightness – the lightness at which the hue is most saturated within the gamut – by 60% of the difference from the base colour (after Judd’s principle of natural colour order); reversed order mirrors the shift.

Source: Classical colour theory (Johannes Itten for RYB); the mappings were developed for Kolorist

Colour semantics and AI

Palettes from value words and “Describe a colour” build on a knowledge base of colour concepts made for the app. The on-device language model interprets the words and chooses hue families and expression. The palette itself is then composed by fixed rules in OKLCH: harmony principle, equal value or equal saturation, one accent, a light or dark background and text with at least 7:1 contrast. Text about colour meaning describes conventions, not scientific facts.

Source: Developed for Kolorist; language model from Apple (Foundation Models)

Light reflectance value and luminance contrast

LRV is the CIE luminance Y in per cent – the share of visible light a surface reflects, as paint manufacturers state it. Kolorist computes it for the colour as shown in sRGB. Contrast between surfaces is given as a difference in LRV points (BS 8300: at least 30) and as luminance contrast (Y₁ − Y₂)/(Y₁ + Y₂) per NS 11001 (0.4 for important surfaces, 0.8 for signage).

Source: CIE 15 (luminance factor Y); BS 8300-2:2018; NS 11001-1:2018 and Byggforsk 220.300

The Munsell system

Hue, value and chroma after Munsell. The conversion uses the 1943 renotation data (CIE xyY under illuminant C, public data) with interpolation in chroma, hue and value, Bradford adaptation to D65, and ASTM D1535 for the relation between value and luminance. The notation is an approximation; physical Munsell chips may differ.

Source: Newhall, Nickerson & Judd: “Final Report of the O.S.A. Subcommittee on the Spacing of the Munsell Colors”, JOSA 33(7), 1943; ASTM D1535

CAM16 and CAT16 – colour appearance in different light

A model of how colours appear under given conditions: the colour of the light, its intensity and the surround. “See in light” first calculates the surface under the light – spectrally when the spectrum of the light is known, using a smooth estimated reflectance spectrum for the colour – and then its appearance in the room, where the eye only partly adapts to the colour of the light and dim light makes colours less colourful. Finally it shows the screen colour with the same lightness and colourfulness. Light compensation uses CAT16 with full adaptation to daylight (D65).

Source: C. Li, Z. Li, Z. Wang et al.: “Comprehensive color solutions: CAM16, CAT16, and CAM16-UCS”, Color Research & Application 42(6), 2017

Spectra, light sources and colour temperature

Light sources are described by CIE spectra (the daylight series, illuminant A, fluorescent and LED types) or as blackbodies. Correlated colour temperature and distance from the Planckian locus (Duv) are calculated after Ohno. Colours without a measured spectrum get the smoothest spectrum that gives the colour in daylight (Burns). Colour rendering is estimated from the reference card’s spectra, compared with a reference light of the same colour temperature; the figure is scaled to lie close to Ra, but is not an official colour rendering index. Standard viewing conditions follow the illuminance in ISO 3664, EN 12464-1 and CIE 157.

Source: CIE 15:2018 Colorimetry; Y. Ohno: “Practical use and calculation of CCT and Duv”, LEUKOS 10(1), 2014; S. A. Burns: “Numerical methods for smoothest reflectance reconstruction”, Color Research & Application 45(1), 2020

Camera characterisation with a reference card

With a reference card in the image, Kolorist fits a tone curve per channel from the grey patches and then a matrix or root polynomial from camera colours to the reference values in daylight – whichever predicts best. Accuracy is given as mean and maximum ΔE00, cross-validated: each patch is predicted by a model fitted without it. A camera profile made once lets a grey card do the job in new light. The card is found automatically in the image (Vision), and the lightness of the patches tells which way round it is. The reference values for the card are not included with the app; you import them yourself.

Source: G. D. Finlayson, M. Mackiewicz, A. Hurlbert: “Color correction using root-polynomial regression”, IEEE TIP 24(5), 2015; ISO 17321-1

Filament colours from FilamentColors.xyz

The built-in filament colours come from FilamentColors.xyz, which prints samples of filament from many manufacturers and measures them with a colorimeter. The values are CIELab under D65 with the 10° observer, as stated in their source code. Kolorist converts Lab to XYZ with the D65 white point for 10° and adapts to 2° with Bradford; the difference between the observers can’t be converted exactly without spectra, but it is small. Older samples without a measurement have a colour from a photograph and are marked as estimated. Each colour links to the sample at FilamentColors.xyz. The extract ships with the app and is updated with new versions; the app fetches nothing from the internet. Photos, purchase links and links to other colour systems are not included. Colours vary between production batches and with surface finish.

Source: FilamentColors.xyz, licensed under CC BY 4.0. Kolorist has selected information and converted the colours, and is not affiliated with FilamentColors.xyz.

About the implementation

The formulas are implemented in the app from the published specifications and papers above, and checked with tests against reference values where available. If you have questions about a method or find something that doesn’t add up, please email eivind.johansen@ntnu.no.
